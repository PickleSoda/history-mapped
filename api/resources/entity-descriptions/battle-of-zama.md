The Battle of Zama was fought in October 202 BC on an open plain in what is now Tunisia, a few days' march inland from Carthage. It was the last great clash of the Second Punic War. A Roman army under Scipio Africanus defeated a Carthaginian army led by Hannibal, and the defeat ended Carthage's ability to go on fighting.

The war had begun in 218 BC with Hannibal's march into Italy. After years of fighting in Italy and Spain, Scipio took the war to Africa and beat two armies there. Carthage called Hannibal home. Negotiations produced a draft treaty, but Carthage dropped it once Hannibal arrived, and Rome answered by taking Carthaginian towns one at a time. Hannibal marched inland for five days and camped about three kilometres from Scipio's camp, hoping to fight before the Numidian horseman Masinissa joined the Romans. Masinissa arrived with several thousand horsemen while the Carthaginians were still on the march. The site is usually placed on the Draa el Metnan plain south of Sicca, modern El Kef, though this is not certain.

The sources give the Romans about 29,000 to 30,000 men, with around 6,000 cavalry, and the Carthaginians 40,000 to 50,000 including 4,000 cavalry and 80 war elephants. Hannibal's army was made up of the survivors of three separate commands, and he kept them in separate lines: elephants and skirmishers in front, then the veterans of Mago's army, then African and Carthaginian levies, and about 200 metres behind them the men who had come back with him from Italy. Scipio put his heavy infantry in three ranks, but lined the maniples up behind each other instead of in a chessboard pattern, so that straight lanes ran through the army. His light infantry used the lanes to slip away from the elephants. The Roman cavalry was on the left under Laelius, and Masinissa's Numidians on the right.

The fighting began with skirmishes between the Numidian horse on the Roman right. Hannibal then sent his elephants forward. The velites threw javelins, horns blared, and many elephants fled, some through their own cavalry. Masinissa and Laelius each used the confusion to charge and drive off the cavalry opposing them, and both chased the beaten horsemen far from the field. The infantry then fought alone. The hastati broke the first Carthaginian line, were thrown back by the second, and were saved when the principes joined in. Scipio recalled his men rather than let them rush on into Hannibal's third line. After a pause in which each side reformed, the two lines fought a long, even struggle until Masinissa and Laelius returned and fell upon the Carthaginian rear. The line collapsed, and a great many were killed or taken. Estimates are around 20,000 killed and as many captured against at least 1,500 Roman dead.

Carthage accepted harsh terms in 201 BC. It lost its overseas empire, gave up its fleet and most of its elephants, paid a large indemnity and could no longer wage war without Roman permission. Scipio took the name Africanus. Historians still debate the exact site, the sizes of the armies, whether Hannibal deliberately tried to lure the Romans forward, and how far his use of elephants was a mistake. Some think he needed time he did not have to train his troops.
