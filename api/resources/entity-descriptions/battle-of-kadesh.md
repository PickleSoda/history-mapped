The Battle of Kadesh was fought in May 1274 BC beside the Orontes river in what is now western Syria, between the Egyptian army of Ramesses II and a Hittite coalition under King Muwatalli II of Hatti. It is often described as the largest chariot battle of the ancient world, with some 2,000 Egyptian chariots and about 2,500 Hittite ones in the field. It matters just as much for the records it left: both sides wrote about it, and the Egyptian version was carved on several temple walls.

The background was a long contest for Syria. After the expulsion of the Hyksos around 1550 BC, the pharaohs of the New Kingdom pushed north as far as the Orontes, but Egyptian influence in the region faded under the late Eighteenth Dynasty. Seti I won back ground, including Kadesh and the kingdom of Amurru, and his son Ramesses campaigned with him. Both places may have drifted back under Hittite control. In his fifth year Ramesses marched from Pi-Ramesses with four divisions named for Amun, Re, Ptah and Seth, and a further contingent, the Ne'arin, was left in Amurru to guard the port of Sumur. Muwatalli's stated aim was to recover Amurru.

About eleven kilometres from Kadesh, Ramesses met two Shasu nomads who told him the Hittite king was still far north near Aleppo. They were Hittite agents. The pharaoh hurried ahead with the Amun division and camped north-west of the town, while the other divisions trailed behind. Only then did scouts bring in two Hittite prisoners who admitted that the whole enemy army lay behind old Kadesh. Before the Ptah and Seth divisions could be brought up, the Hittite chariots crossed the Orontes and hit the Re division as it marched in column, scattering it. The attackers swept round the camp and broke through the Amun shield wall.

The Hittite charge lost momentum among the tents and baggage, and many of the chariots stopped to plunder. Ramesses, by his own account abandoned by his officers, rallied his guard, some Amun chariots and the survivors of Re, and drove the attackers back toward the river. Muwatalli sent in about a thousand more chariots, but the Ne'arin from Amurru arrived and surprised them, and the Ptah division threatened their rear. After six charges in all the survivors were pinned against the river, abandoned their chariots and swam, and many drowned. The Hittite infantry stayed unengaged across the river.

The outcome was inconclusive. Ramesses could not besiege the walled town, withdrew south, and advertised a personal triumph. Hittite records instead say he was forced to leave, and Muwatalli went on to take the Egyptian province of Upi. Modern historians treat it as a draw. Ramesses took Dapur and Tunip in his eighth and ninth years, but the gains did not last, and neither empire ever beat the other decisively. About fifteen years later, in 1258 BC, Ramesses and the Hittite king Hattusili III signed a treaty. A clay copy of it was found at the Hittite capital. How many troops fought, how the arrivals were timed, and how much of the Egyptian account to trust remain open questions.
