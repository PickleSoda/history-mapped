The Battle of Vienna was fought on 12 September 1683 on the slopes and villages north-west of the Habsburg capital. It ended a two-month siege of the city by an Ottoman army under Grand Vizier Kara Mustafa Pasha and pitted that army, with its Crimean Tatar, Wallachian, Moldavian and Transylvanian auxiliaries, against a relief force of Poles under King John III Sobieski, Imperial troops under Charles of Lorraine, and German contingents from Saxony, Bavaria and other princes. The Christian coalition won a decisive victory.

The background lay in two decades of uneasy peace after the Peace of Vasvár and in unrest in Habsburg Hungary, where Imre Thököly led rebels who received Ottoman support. The Ottoman court mobilised in January 1682 and declared war in August; the main army marched from Edirne in 1683, reached Belgrade in May, and arrived before Vienna on 14 July after Emperor Leopold had fled to Passau. The long gap before the invasion gave the Habsburgs time to ally with Poland through the Treaty of Warsaw and to gather money, with large sums from the pope. Count Starhemberg held the city with a garrison and militia, while Ottoman sappers dug mines toward the bastions.

On the day itself the Ottomans attacked at four in the morning, hoping to disturb the allied deployment on the Kahlenberg slopes. The Germans answered first: Charles of Lorraine's Imperial troops took Nussdorf and Heiligenstadt after heavy back-and-forth fighting. At midday the mines under the Löbelbastei were found and disarmed, and Cantacuzino's Wallachians, who sympathised with the allies, abandoned the bridge on Brigittenau island. In the early afternoon Polish infantry advanced on the other side of the field and took Gersthof by four o'clock, while Saxons, Bavarians and Austrians closed on the Türkenschanze and took the Döbling villages by five. A probing charge by about 120 hussars showed the Ottoman line was fragile, and Kara Mustafa fell back to his camp.

At about six in the evening Sobieski sent four cavalry contingents, three Polish and one Imperial, about 18,000 horsemen in all, down the hills. He led some 3,000 Winged Hussars himself, and the exhausted Ottoman line broke. The cavalry rode on to the Ottoman camp, and the Viennese garrison sallied out to join in. In under three hours the siege was lifted. Sobieski later reworked Caesar's boast into "We came, we saw, God conquered".

The victory ended the Ottoman threat to central Europe in this form and opened the Habsburg counter-offensive in Hungary, under the Holy League with Poland, Venice and the papacy, which gradually recovered the kingdom over the following years. Kara Mustafa was executed at the end of 1683. Casualty figures vary widely, from roughly 8,000 to 20,000 Ottoman dead in the battle and about 4,500 on the allied side, with many civilians killed or enslaved. Historians also argue over the true size of the armies and how much the Crimean Tatars fought in the final battle.
