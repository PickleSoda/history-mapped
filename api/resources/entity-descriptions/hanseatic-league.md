The Hanseatic League, or Hansa, was a network of German merchant guilds and trading towns that dominated the sea trade of the Baltic and the North Sea from the thirteenth to the fifteenth century. Led by Lübeck, it linked nearly two hundred towns at its widest, from Bruges and Cologne in the west to Riga, Reval and Novgorod in the east, yet it never had a standing army, a permanent government or a common treasury. Its strength lay in shared privileges abroad, in the readiness of its towns to act together, and in their command of the shipping between east and west.

The league grew out of the associations, or hansas, that German merchants formed to trade overseas. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries traders from Lübeck joined the Gotlanders of Visby in the trade to Novgorod, the great market of the Russian north, and German towns were founded or settled along the eastern Baltic. In 1241 Lübeck allied with Hamburg, joining the Baltic to the North Sea by a short land route, and in 1266 and 1282 the merchants won privileges and a common house in London. By the middle of the fourteenth century the four great Kontore, the privileged trading houses at Novgorod, Bergen, Bruges and London, were organised corporations, and diets of the towns met at Lübeck.

Hanseatic ships, first cogs and later hulks, carried furs, wax, grain, timber, flax and pitch from the east to Flanders and England, and brought cloth and salt back. Herring from the Sound, stockfish from Bergen and the beer of the Wendish towns were staples of the trade. The towns enforced their interests with embargoes, boycotts and, when needed, war: after King Valdemar IV of Denmark seized Visby in 1361, the towns combined in the Confederation of Cologne, sacked Copenhagen and in the Peace of Stralsund of 1370 won control of the Sound fortresses for fifteen years. That treaty marked the height of Hanseatic power.

The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries brought rivals the league could not master. Dutch shippers, with lower costs, won free access to the Baltic in 1441; territorial princes curbed the independence of the towns; Ivan III closed the Novgorod Kontor in 1494; and the growth of Atlantic trade drew business to other ports. The Bruges Kontor moved to Antwerp in 1520 and closed in 1593, and Elizabeth I shut the London Steelyard in 1598. The Thirty Years' War completed the decline, and the last diet met in 1669. Lübeck, Hamburg and Bremen kept the name of Hanseatic cities, and the Hansa's brick churches, warehouses and the wharf of Bryggen in Bergen still mark the towns it joined.
