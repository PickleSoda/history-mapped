The Battle of Waterloo was fought on Sunday 18 June 1815 on the rolling farmland around Mont-Saint-Jean, some fifteen kilometres south of Brussels, in what was then the United Kingdom of the Netherlands and is now Belgium. Napoleon's Armée du Nord attacked the Duke of Wellington's army of British, King's German Legion, Dutch-Belgian, Hanoverian, Brunswick and Nassau troops, and was routed in the evening after the Prussian army of Field Marshal Blücher came in on its flank. The French called it the battle of Mont-Saint-Jean and the Prussians named it after the inn of La Belle Alliance. It was Napoleon's last battle: he abdicated four days later, ending the Hundred Days and the First French Empire.

Napoleon had left Elba and retaken power in March 1815, and the powers of the Seventh Coalition declared him an outlaw and began to mobilise against him. Rather than wait for their armies to converge on France, he struck first at the two nearest, Wellington's and Blücher's, which were quartered across Belgium. He crossed the frontier at Charleroi on 15 June and beat the Prussians at Ligny on the 16th while Marshal Ney fought Wellington at Quatre Bras. The Prussians then fell back north to Wavre instead of east toward their own bases, which kept them within a day's march of Wellington, who withdrew to a ridge he had surveyed the year before. Napoleon sent Marshal Grouchy with about 33,000 men after the Prussians, and that force spent the 18th fighting their rearguard at Wavre.

Wellington kept most of his infantry behind the crest of the Mont-Saint-Jean ridge, out of sight of the French guns, and garrisoned the château of Hougoumont, the farm of La Haye Sainte and the hamlets around Papelotte in front of it. Napoleon waited until late morning for the rain-soaked ground to dry. The struggle for Hougoumont, begun around 11:30, swallowed most of Reille's corps for the rest of the day. At about 13:30 d'Erlon's corps attacked the allied left in dense columns and was broken by Picton's infantry and the charge of the Household and Union cavalry brigades, which then galloped on into the French guns and were cut to pieces in turn. From about 16:00 Ney flung massed cuirassiers at the allied centre, where the infantry formed squares and held. Meanwhile Bülow's Prussian IV Corps emerged from the Bois de Paris and fought for the village of Plancenoit behind the French right, drawing off Napoleon's reserves.

Around 18:30 the French captured La Haye Sainte and pushed guns close to Wellington's centre, which came near to breaking. At about 19:30 Napoleon sent battalions of his Imperial Guard up the slope; they were stopped by Maitland's Foot Guards, Chassé's Dutch-Belgian division and a flank attack by Colborne's 52nd Light Infantry. As the Guard fell back, Zieten's Prussian I Corps broke in near Papelotte, the Prussians stormed Plancenoit, and the French army fell apart. Wellington and Blücher met near La Belle Alliance, and Prussian cavalry hunted the fugitives through the night as far as Genappe. The French lost about 25,000 killed and wounded and thousands of prisoners; Wellington's army lost some 17,000 and the Prussians about 7,000.

Waterloo closed more than two decades of war between revolutionary and Napoleonic France and the rest of Europe and opened a long period of relative peace shaped by the Congress of Vienna. Napoleon surrendered to the Royal Navy in July and died in exile on Saint Helena in 1821. How much of the victory belongs to Wellington's army and how much to the Prussians has been argued since 1815, as have the late start, Grouchy's absence, Ney's unsupported cavalry charges and the exact course of the Guard's last attack. Wellington himself called the day "the nearest-run thing you ever saw in your life". The field, overlooked since the 1820s by the Lion's Mound, still keeps the farms of Hougoumont, La Haye Sainte, Papelotte and La Belle Alliance.
