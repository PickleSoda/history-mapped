The Battle of Manzikert was fought on 26 August 1071 near the town now called Malazgirt, in the highlands north of Lake Van in eastern Anatolia. The Byzantine emperor Romanos IV Diogenes met the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan, and the day ended in a Seljuk victory and the capture of the emperor himself. The professional core of the Byzantine field army was destroyed.

Romanos came to the frontier to deal with Turkic raiding and settlement in the east. His army was large and mixed: regulars from the western and eastern provinces, retinues and levies of the border lords, Varangians, Frankish and Norman mercenaries under Roussel de Bailleul, Georgians, Armenians, Bulgarians and Turkic mercenaries. Sources put it at around 40,000 men, while some chroniclers claim far more. Its march across Asia Minor was long and hard, and the emperor's luxurious baggage train and his Franks' plundering did little for morale. Back in Constantinople, the Doukas family were his political rivals, and one of them, Andronikos, travelled with the army.

Near the end of the march Romanos split his force. Joseph Tarchaniotes was sent with part of the regulars, the Varangians and the Pecheneg and Frankish mercenaries towards Khliat, while the emperor went on to Manzikert, which he took on 23 August. What happened to Tarchaniotes is unclear: Islamic sources say Alp Arslan crushed him, Byzantine sources say nothing of it, and one says he fled. Either way, the army at Manzikert was about half the planned size. Alp Arslan, with perhaps 35,000 to 50,000 men including Kurdish allies, had scouts who knew exactly where the Byzantines were, while Romanos knew nothing of the Seljuks. On 24 August foragers under Bryennios met the Seljuks, a cavalry detachment under Basilakes was routed and its leader taken, and the army's Turkic mercenaries began to defect. Romanos refused a peace offer, wanting a decisive result.

On 26 August the Byzantines formed up with Bryennios on the left, Theodore Alyates on the right, the emperor in the centre and Andronikos Doukas commanding the reserve. The Seljuks waited about four kilometres away in a crescent. As the Byzantines advanced the Seljuk centre fell back while the wings closed round them, and horse archers poured in arrows. The Byzantines took Alp Arslan's camp by late afternoon, but the Seljuks simply avoided a close fight. At dusk Romanos ordered a retreat. The right wing misunderstood, and Doukas ignored the order and withdrew to the camp instead of covering the army. The Seljuks attacked; the right wing broke, the left under Bryennios soon followed, and the centre with the emperor and the Varangians was surrounded. Romanos was wounded and captured. Accounts disagree on who fled first, and on whether the Armenians or the Turkish auxiliaries stood their ground.

Alp Arslan treated the captive emperor with some kindness and, after about a week, agreed terms: the surrender of Antioch, Edessa, Hierapolis and Manzikert, a ransom paid partly at once and partly by yearly instalments, and a marriage alliance. Back in the empire, Romanos was beaten three times by the Doukas faction, deposed, blinded and exiled, and he died soon after. The defeat itself cost relatively few lives compared with its effects: the empire lost its best troops, civil strife followed, and Turkic groups gradually settled across Anatolia. Historians still debate how much the battle itself, as opposed to the political collapse after it, brought about that change.
