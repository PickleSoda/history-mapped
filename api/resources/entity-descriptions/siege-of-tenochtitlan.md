name: Siege of Tenochtitlan
---
The siege of Tenochtitlan lasted from late May to 13 August 1521. It ended with the fall of the Mexica capital, an island city in Lake Texcoco, to Hernan Cortes and an enormous body of native allies, chiefly Tlaxcalans, along with men of Texcoco, Chalco and Huejotzingo. The Spanish dated the surrender to 13 August, when the last Mexica ruler Cuauhtemoc was captured on the lake. The fall effectively ended the Aztec Empire and became the base for New Spain.

The background was the collapse of Spanish fortunes in 1520. After the massacre at the Toxcatl festival and the uprising that followed, the Spaniards were driven out of the city on the Noche Triste and fought their way to Tlaxcala. Smallpox then swept the valley and killed many people, including the ruler Cuitlahuac, who was succeeded by Cuauhtemoc. Cortes decided on a different method: instead of assaulting the capital again he would cut it off. He had Martin Lopez build thirteen brigantines, moved to Texcoco, replaced its ruler with the friendly Ixtlilxochitl, and brought over lakeshore towns. By May he counted roughly 84 horsemen, about 194 crossbowmen and arquebusiers and 650 foot, together with allied armies which the sources put anywhere from 50,000 to 200,000.

On 22 May the army was split. Alvarado went to Tacuba, Olid to Coyoacan and Sandoval to Iztapalapa, each with about 8,000 allies, while Cortes took the fleet. The first act was to cut the aqueduct from Chapultepec. Early attacks on the causeways were thrown back, but the brigantines, helped by a favourable wind, broke the canoe fleet, and openings cut in the causeways let ships protect the flanks of the troops. The Mexica answered with pits, hidden stakes and ambushes, and on 24 June Cuauhtemoc attacked all camps at once. Alvarado was wounded, and Cortes himself was nearly captured at a canal he had failed to fill; dozens of Spanish prisoners were sacrificed within sight of the camps.

After this setback many allies left, but returned when the prophecy of Spanish defeat failed. Cortes then advanced slowly, razing each quarter and using the rubble to fill gaps so that cavalry and infantry could fight in order. Food and clean water ran out in the city, and disease and dysentery spread. In early August the three divisions converged on the Tlatelolco market, the great temple there was burned, and the remaining defenders were crowded into a shrinking pocket. When Cuauhtemoc tried to flee by canoe he was seized by Sandoval's brigantines.

The cost was immense. Estimates of the dead for the campaign range from 100,000 to 240,000, with tens of thousands of civilians killed or captured, and most of the nobility perished. Allied warriors continued killing and looting after the surrender, which Cortes himself said he struggled to prevent. Cuauhtemoc was tortured over the missing gold and later hanged in 1525. Numbers of every kind are disputed, since the main accounts come from Cortes, Bernal Diaz and other Spanish writers, and the exact routes of the causeways are reconstructed from a landscape now drained and built over.
