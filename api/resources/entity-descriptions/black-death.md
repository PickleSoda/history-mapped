The Black Death was the pandemic of bubonic and pneumonic plague that swept across western Eurasia and North Africa between 1346 and 1353. Caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by the fleas of rats and other animals and, in its pneumonic form, passed between people, it was the deadliest pandemic in recorded history. Estimates of the dead run from a quarter to well over half of Europe's population, perhaps 25 to 50 million people there, with about a third of the Middle East's people dying as well.

The first clear reports come from the Golden Horde in 1346, when the disease broke out on the lower Volga and in the army of Khan Jani Beg besieging Caffa, the Genoese trading colony in the Crimea. Where it came from before that is still argued. Gravestones near Lake Issyk-Kul in Central Asia record a pestilence in 1338 and 1339, and plague DNA recovered from them belongs to the strain ancestral to the Black Death; other historians look to the steppe north-west of the Caspian or to regions further west.

Italian merchant shipping carried the plague out of the Black Sea. It reached Constantinople in the summer of 1347, Sicily and Alexandria that autumn, and Genoa, Venice, Pisa and Marseille in the first weeks of 1348. In the course of 1348 it ran through Italy, France, Iberia, Egypt and Syria, killing perhaps a third or more of Cairo's population and about half of Paris's, and landed in England at Melcombe in June. It crossed the German lands and reached Norway in 1349, swept the Baltic coasts in 1350, and arrived in Pskov and Novgorod in 1352 and Moscow in 1353.

Contemporaries had no explanation that helped. Physicians such as Guy de Chauliac, who stayed with Pope Clement VI in Avignon, described the disease carefully but could not cure it. Many saw it as divine punishment; flagellants marched from town to town, and Jews were accused of poisoning wells and massacred, from Strasbourg in February 1349 to Mainz and Cologne that August, despite the pope's bulls condemning the killings; by 1351 some two hundred Jewish communities had been destroyed.

The loss of so many people reshaped the late medieval world. Labour became scarce, wages rose and landlords tried to hold them down, and abandoned villages returned to pasture or forest. Plague kept returning every few years for centuries, and Europe's population did not regain its early fourteenth-century level until the sixteenth century. The Black Death is counted as the start of the second plague pandemic, which lasted into the early nineteenth century.
