The Battle of Issus was fought on 5 November 333 BC on a narrow coastal plain beside the Gulf of Issus, in what is now southern Turkey. Alexander of Macedon, at the head of Macedonian and allied Greek troops, defeated the Persian army led in person by the Great King Darius III. It was Alexander's second great pitched battle in Asia, after the Granicus the year before, and the first time a Persian army had been beaten with its king on the field.

The campaign had been shaped by the sea. Alexander had spent 334 and early 333 BC taking the Aegean and southern coast of Asia Minor, aiming to deny the stronger Persian fleet its harbours. While he was at Tarsus, he learned that Darius was gathering a large army in the east. Alexander sent Parmenion ahead to hold the coast near Issus, then moved the rest of the army toward the Pass of Jonah. Darius knew the pass was held and chose a northern route round the Amanus mountains. His army took Issus without a fight and so lay behind the Macedonians, across their supply line. Alexander turned his army back, and Darius moved south until scouts found the Macedonians approaching along the shore, so that he had to form up where he stood, at a river generally called the Pinarus.

The ground favoured the smaller army. Between the gulf and the foothills the plain was only about 2.6 km across, which denied Darius the room to use his numbers. Scholars still argue about which stream was the Pinarus. Older writers chose the Deli Cay, while Hammond and Devine, using Callisthenes' distances and the lie of the land, argue for the Payas, and this story follows the Payas. Alexander's army is put at about 37,000 men, with some 5,000 cavalry. Ancient writers gave Darius between 250,000 and 600,000 men, but modern historians usually settle on a total of not more than about 100,000, and some have gone much lower. His line included heavy cavalry by the sea, a body of Greek mercenaries in the centre, and Persian infantry reaching into the hills so as to bend back toward Alexander's right.

The battle went in three phases. On the Macedonian left, Parmenion and the Thessalian horse faced a heavy cavalry charge and held by repeated counter-charges. In the centre the phalanx, advancing across a stream and up a steep bank, was thrown back by the mercenaries, and about 120 Macedonians of rank fell. On the right, Alexander led the hypaspists against the Persian infantry, and the Agrianians pushed back the skirmishers in the foothills. Alexander then went at the Great King with the Companion cavalry. Darius fled, and Alexander turned on the flank and rear of the mercenaries rather than chase him at once. When the Persian soldiers saw that the king had gone, the rest of the line gave way, and the cavalry hunted the crowded fugitives until darkness.

The consequences were large. The Macedonians took Darius's mother, wife and daughters, whom Alexander treated with consideration, and the royal baggage. Persian forces were hurt but their empire was not yet broken, and Darius returned to raise another army, which Alexander faced at Gaugamela two years later. Meanwhile Alexander went south through Phoenicia to take the ports on which the Persian fleet depended. Mercenaries who escaped from Issus later joined the Spartan king Agis III in his unsuccessful revolt against Macedon. Alexander's own losses are given as about 150 killed, with thousands wounded, while Persian casualties are put in the tens of thousands, and all these figures are ancient or heavily estimated.
