name: Battle of Red Cliffs
---
The Battle of Red Cliffs was fought in the winter of 208 AD on the Yangtze River in what is now Hubei province, China. An alliance of the southern warlord Sun Quan, the wandering commander Liu Bei and Liu Qi, the son of the late governor of Jingzhou, defeated the much larger army of Cao Cao. The victory stopped Cao Cao from crossing the river and taking the south, and it opened the way to the division of the old Han empire into the Three Kingdoms.

The Han dynasty was nearly four centuries old and its emperor, Xian, was a figurehead who held no real power. Cao Cao hosted him at Ye and ruled as the most powerful warlord. After beating Yuan Shao at Guandu in 200, he controlled the North China Plain. In 208 he marched south, took over Jingzhou, and moved down the Yangtze against Sun Quan and Liu Bei, who agreed to resist together. Zhou Yu commanded the allied forces, and Zhuge Liang is named as one of their commanders.

The allied fleet sailed upstream from Xiakou or Fankou and met Cao Cao's vanguard at Red Cliffs. Cao Cao's men were sick and demoralised after forced marches, and they lost the small fight. Cao Cao pulled back to Wulin on the north bank and the allies returned to the south bank. Most of Cao Cao's soldiers were northerners with little experience of boats, and he is said to have chained his ships stem to stern, perhaps to reduce seasickness. The allied general Huang Gai wrote a letter to Cao Cao offering to defect and prepared warships filled with kindling, dry reeds and oil. The squadron sailed to mid-river, the crews lit the ships and left in small boats, and the south-east wind carried the burning hulls into Cao Cao's fleet. The fire spread, and Zhou Yu followed with a lightly armed force. Cao Cao ordered a retreat and destroyed some of his remaining ships.

The retreat took the northern army along the Huarong road, through marshes made worse by heavy rain. Sick soldiers carried grass to fill the mud for the horses, and many drowned or were trampled. Hunger and disease did more damage while the allies chased by land and water to Nan Commandery. Cao Cao escaped through Huarong Pass and went back north, leaving Cao Ren and Xu Huang at Jiangling, Yue Jin at Xiangyang and Man Chong at Dangyang. Chaos at the river crossings slowed the allied pursuit, and Cao Ren's rearguard prevented a worse disaster.

By the end of 209 Zhou Yu took Jiangling, and Cao Cao's border shrank about 160 kilometres to the area of Xiangyang. Liu Qi died within months, Sun Quan absorbed most of his lands, and Liu Bei took four commanderies south of the Yangtze. Zhou Yu died in 210. Cao Cao never again commanded so large a fleet. Strength figures are uncertain: Cao Cao claimed 800,000 men, while Zhou Yu estimated 220,000 to 240,000, against about 50,000 allies. The battlefield itself is still debated, with Chibi, Jiayu, Wuhan and Huangzhou all proposed, and the ancient river course has shifted since.
