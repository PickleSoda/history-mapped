The Battle of Hastings was fought on 14 October 1066 on a ridge about 11 kilometres north-west of Hastings in Sussex, where the town of Battle now stands. King Harold II of England, with an army of household troops and shire levies fighting on foot, held the hilltop for most of a day against William, Duke of Normandy, whose army of Normans, Bretons, Frenchmen and Flemings had landed on the south coast a fortnight earlier. By nightfall Harold and two of his brothers were dead, the English army had been destroyed, and the way lay open for William to take the English crown.

The crisis began with the death of the childless Edward the Confessor in January 1066. Harold Godwinson, the most powerful earl in the kingdom, was crowned the next day, but William insisted that Edward had promised him the succession and that Harold had sworn to uphold it, and Harald Hardrada of Norway put forward a claim of his own. Harold guarded the south coast through the summer until his levies ran out of supplies and went home in early September. Soon afterwards Hardrada and Harold's exiled brother Tostig landed in the north; Harold marched to Yorkshire and annihilated them at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. Three days later William came ashore at Pevensey, moved to Hastings and fortified a camp. Harold raced back through London and on the evening of 13 October gathered his men at the hoary apple tree, a landmark just north of the ridge.

The English formed a dense shield wall along the crest, with the professional housecarls around the king's standards and the less heavily armed fyrd on the wings. William drew up in three divisions, Bretons on the left, Normans in the centre and French and Flemings on the right, each with archers in front, armoured foot behind and mounted knights at the rear. From about nine in the morning his archers, infantry and cavalry went up the slope in turn and made no impression on the wall. Later in the morning the Breton wing broke and fled, part of the English right ran down the hill after it, and a rumour that the duke had been killed swept his army. William checked the panic by baring his head to show he was alive, and his knights rode down the English who had left the ridge, killing them on a small hillock.

The Normans then made a weapon of that near disaster: according to William of Poitiers, twice that afternoon bodies of horsemen pretended to flee and then turned on the English who followed them. Together with repeated archery and assaults, these losses wore the shield wall down until it could barely cover the crest. Late in the day Harold was killed. The Bayeux Tapestry famously shows a warrior with an arrow at his eye, though whether that figure is the king is still disputed. The English then broke; the royal household died around his body and the rest fled north in the dusk, and at a ravine remembered as the Malfosse a rearguard turned on the pursuing knights and wounded Eustace of Boulogne before it too was overwhelmed.

Hastings did not end English resistance at once, but no army could be raised to match William's. He advanced on London, accepted the submission of the leading men at Berkhamsted and was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066, and the risings of the next five years were crushed with great severity. The conquest replaced the English aristocracy with a Norman one and reshaped the church, government and language of England. William founded Battle Abbey on the battlefield, its high altar said to stand where Harold fell. Caldbec Hill and Crowhurst have been proposed as alternative sites, but most historians still accept the abbey ridge; the size of the armies, the timing of the feigned flights and the manner of Harold's death remain open questions.
