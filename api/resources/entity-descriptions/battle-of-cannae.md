The Battle of Cannae was fought on 2 August 216 BC beside the river Aufidus, the modern Ofanto, below the hilltop town of Cannae in Apulia, south-eastern Italy. In the third year of the Second Punic War a Carthaginian army of about 50,000 under Hannibal met a Roman and allied army of roughly 86,000 commanded by the consuls Gaius Terentius Varro and Lucius Aemilius Paullus. By letting the Roman infantry drive into the centre of his line and then closing in on its flanks and rear, Hannibal surrounded and destroyed most of the army in front of him. It was one of the heaviest defeats Rome ever suffered and remains the textbook example of a double envelopment.

Hannibal had crossed the Alps in 218 BC and crushed Roman armies at the Trebia and at Lake Trasimene. Through 217 BC the dictator Quintus Fabius Maximus refused to give him another pitched battle and wore him down instead, but that cautious strategy was unpopular. For 216 BC Rome raised eight legions, matched them with as many allied troops, and sent both consuls out together to force a decision. Early that summer Hannibal seized the supply depot on the hill of Cannae near the Adriatic coast. The Romans followed and camped on both banks of the river, a few kilometres from his own camp. The consuls held command on alternate days, and the ancient writers paint Paullus as prudent and Varro as headstrong, a contrast that may owe something to the search for someone to blame.

On Varro's day of command the Romans crossed to the south-eastern bank and drew up on a narrow plain between the river and the high ground of Cannae. Counting on sheer weight, they packed their infantry into maniples far deeper than usual on a short front, with the citizen cavalry beside the river and the allied cavalry on the other wing. Hannibal set his Spanish and Gallic heavy cavalry under Hasdrubal against the Romans by the river and his Numidian light horse on the other flank. His centre of Gallic and Spanish foot was pushed forward in a shallow arc, and his best troops, the African heavy infantry, stood back on either side of it.

Hasdrubal's horsemen swiftly destroyed the Roman cavalry along the river while the Numidians kept the allied cavalry occupied. As the legions pressed into the bulging centre, the Gauls and Spaniards gave ground in good order until their line had sagged into a hollow, drawing the Romans ever deeper. The African infantry then turned inward against both Roman flanks, and Hasdrubal, after riding behind the Roman army to scatter the allied horse, struck the Roman rear. Hemmed in and so crowded that most men could not use their weapons, the Romans were cut down through the afternoon. Paullus died on the field, and Varro escaped to Venusia. Polybius gives some 70,000 Roman dead and Livy about 48,000, while Hannibal lost between 5,700 and 8,000 men, most of them Gauls.

The defeat drove Capua and much of southern Italy over to Hannibal and encouraged Philip V of Macedon to ally with him, yet Rome refused to negotiate. It raised fresh legions, went back to avoiding pitched battles in Italy, and outlasted him; fourteen years later Scipio, who had survived Cannae as a young officer, defeated Hannibal at Zama. Later commanders and staff planners, among them Alfred von Schlieffen, studied Cannae as the model of a battle of annihilation. Much is still debated: which bank of the river the armies fought on and how far its course has since shifted, the true size of both armies and of the Roman losses, and how the Roman calendar date of 2 August translates into the solar year.
