The Battle of Agincourt was fought on 25 October 1415, the feast of Saints Crispin and Crispinian, near the village of Azincourt in Artois, northern France. It set the army of Henry V of England against a much larger French host led by the Constable, Charles d'Albret, and ended in a decisive English victory. Modern estimates put the English at roughly 6,000 to 8,500 men, most of them archers, and the French at 14,000 to 15,000, or up to 25,000 if armed servants are counted.

Henry had landed in Normandy in August 1415 after years of failed negotiations over his claim to the French crown, his demands for lost territory and the dowry for Catherine of Valois. His army besieged Harfleur, which fell on 22 September, but the long siege and disease cost him many men. Late in the season he set out for Calais, and the French, who had been slow to gather, finally got ahead of him and blocked the road near Azincourt. The English spent a wet night near Maisoncelle, and Henry ordered silence in the camp.

The ground decided much of the day. The field was a ploughed strip, softened by rain, squeezed between the woods of Azincourt and Tramecourt, and about 690 metres wide. Henry drew up some 1,500 men-at-arms four deep in three battles, with the longbowmen on the flanks driving sharpened stakes into the earth. The French crowded their lords into a dense dismounted vanguard and main battle, with cavalry on the flanks, and had so little room that the crossbowmen and archers were pushed to the rear. For about three hours neither side moved, since the French expected reinforcements and had no need to attack. Henry, whose men were hungry and tired, advanced his line, re-planted the stakes within range and opened fire.

The French cavalry charged in ragged order and failed against stakes and woods. Wounded horses fled back through the French foot, churning the mud. The dismounted men-at-arms then struggled forward in heavy plate, reached the English line and were pushed into an ever tighter press, unable to use their weapons well. English archers, who could move easily in light kit, attacked them on the flanks with swords, hatchets and mallets. The second French line joined and was swallowed in turn. A local knight, Ysembart d'Azincourt, raided the English baggage, though sources disagree about when. Late in the fight Henry, fearing that the French rearguard was reforming and that his prisoners might rearm, ordered most of them killed. English knights refused to carry this out, and the order was later rescinded.

Thousands of French died, among them d'Albret and the dukes of Alencon and Bar, and between 700 and 2,200 were captured. English dead are put at up to 600, including the Duke of York. Henry reached Calais and went home a national hero, and the victory strengthened the position that led to the Treaty of Troyes in 1420. Debate continues over the exact battlefield, with Michael Livingston arguing for ground west of the village, over the true numbers, over how far longbows could pierce plate, and over the scale and motive of the killing of prisoners.
