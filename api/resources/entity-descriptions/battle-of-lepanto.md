name: Battle of Lepanto

On 7 October 1571 a fleet of the Holy League met the Ottoman navy at the mouth of the Gulf of Patras, off the western coast of Greece. The League was an alliance of Spain, Venice, the Papal States, Genoa and several smaller Italian powers and the Knights of Malta, and it was commanded by John of Austria, the young half-brother of Philip II of Spain. In roughly four hours of close combat between oared galleys, the last great fleet action of its kind, the Christian side destroyed or captured most of the Ottoman fleet and killed its admiral, Ali Pasha.

The League had been assembled by Pope Pius V after the Ottoman conquest of Cyprus, with Venice desperate for help. Its galleys, about 212 in all, sailed from Messina in September, crossing the Adriatic amid quarrels between Venetians and Spaniards, among them the hanging of a Spanish soldier by Sebastiano Venier. Learning that the Ottomans lay at Lepanto, the fleet reached Kefalonia on 6 October. Some captains, Gianandrea Doria among them, wished to avoid a battle, but John of Austria insisted on fighting. On the Ottoman side Mehmed Sirocco advised waiting under the fortress, while others, trusting their numbers and the sultan's orders, urged an attack, and Ali Pasha agreed.

The League drew up in four divisions, with galleys of all nations mixed together and Spanish and Italian infantry on every ship, a lesson of the defeat at Preveza in 1538. Agostino Barbarigo commanded the left near the northern shore, John of Austria the centre, Doria the right to the south, and Alvaro de Bazan a reserve of 38 galleys behind. Six Venetian galleasses, big gun platforms, were towed ahead of the line. The Ottomans formed a crescent: Sirocco on their right, Ali Pasha in the centre, and the Algerian-based corsair Uluc Ali on the left, with a small reserve. The wind turned in the League's favour around noon, and the galleasses broke up the leading Turkish ships.

In the north Barbarigo tried to keep close to the shore, but Sirocco slipped galleys between him and the coast. Barbarigo was killed by an arrow, yet Bazan's reserve helped to restore the line, Sirocco died, and freed slaves joined the fighting. In the centre the flagships collided, and the Real was nearly taken until Marcantonio Colonna came alongside; Ali Pasha was killed and the League banner was raised on his captured galley. In the south Doria sailed away from the centre, perhaps to avoid being outflanked, and Uluc Ali fell on the gap near the Maltese flagship until the reserve arrived. He retreated with the Maltese standard. The League took 117 galleys and sank or destroyed about 83 others, freed thousands of Christian oarsmen, and lost around 7,500 dead by the more cautious figures.

Lepanto was a great moral victory for Christian Europe and was celebrated in painting, poetry and church feasts. Its strategic results were modest: the Ottomans rebuilt their fleet within a year, and the alliance soon broke up, with Venice making peace in 1573. Casualty estimates differ widely, from 7,500 to 10,000 Christian and 20,000 to 40,000 Ottoman dead, and the exact positions at sea, the reasons for Doria's movement and the details of the reserve's actions are known only from narrative accounts.
