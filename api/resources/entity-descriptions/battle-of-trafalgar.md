On 21 October 1805 a British fleet under Vice-Admiral Lord Nelson destroyed the combined French and Spanish fleet off the Atlantic coast of Spain, west of Cadiz and close to Cape Trafalgar. Nelson had twenty-seven ships of the line, about 17,000 men and some 2,100 guns. Against him Vice-Admiral Villeneuve commanded thirty-three ships of the line, among them the largest ships then afloat, with around 30,000 men. The British lost no ship, while the allies lost nearly all they had at sea that day.

The background was a long game of cat and mouse. Nelson had pursued Villeneuve, and the allied fleet had ended up in Cadiz, where a British force blockaded it, and Villeneuve was worn down by the chase. Nelson briefed his captains on a plan that rejected the accepted practice of sailing in parallel lines and trading broadsides, which usually ended in drawn battles. Instead he would cut the enemy line in three with two columns sailing at right angles to it, one at the rear under Collingwood and one at the centre under himself, and create a close melee in which his crews' gunnery and speed would decide the day.

The plan carried a plain danger. While approaching head on, the leading ships could be raked by broadsides they could not answer. In the light winds that morning the approach was very slow, and the leading ships were under fire for a long time before their own guns bore. Royal Sovereign, with a freshly cleaned bottom, outsailed the rest and broke the line astern of the Spanish flagship Santa Ana at around midday. At about a quarter to one Victory passed between Bucentaure and Redoutable and fired a devastating raking broadside into Villeneuve's flagship. Victory then locked with Redoutable, and a French marksman in the mizzen top shot Nelson, who died about three hours later, at half past four. Redoutable surrendered at five to two, Santisima Trinidad gave up after three hours of beating, and the allied van, barely engaged, finally turned away under Rear-Admiral Dumanoir.

The British took twenty ships and lost none, but a gale followed. Many prizes were wrecked, sank or were scuttled, and the French and Spanish retook a few. Eleven allied ships got back to Cadiz, and on 23 October a sortie under Commodore Cosmao tried to recover prizes, retaking Santa Ana and Neptuno amid the storm, though Collingwood formed a line and no major action followed. Dumanoir's four ships were caught and taken at Cape Ortegal on 4 November. Allied casualties were about 4,400 killed and 2,500 wounded, with 7,000 to 8,000 captured, against 458 British killed and 1,208 wounded.

Trafalgar confirmed British command of the sea and ended serious French hopes of challenging it at sea. Nelson's death made him a national hero. The exact details of the fight remain debated, since the smoke, light wind and loss of records make ship-by-ship timings uncertain, and later accounts differ on who first proposed the Cadiz sortie.
