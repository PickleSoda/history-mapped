The Battle of Salamis was a naval battle fought in late September 480 BC in the narrow strait between the island of Salamis and the coast of Attica, a few kilometres west of Athens. The fleet of the allied Greek city-states, led in practice by the Athenian Themistocles and nominally by the Spartan Eurybiades, defeated the much larger fleet of the Persian king Xerxes I. Some 370 Greek triremes, more than half of them Athenian, sank or captured perhaps 200 to 300 enemy ships for the loss of about 40, and the battle became the turning point of the Persian invasion of Greece.

Xerxes had crossed into Europe in the spring of 480 BC with an army and fleet meant to finish what his father Darius had begun at Marathon ten years earlier. After the Greeks lost the pass of Thermopylae and gave up the strait of Artemisium, the Persians overran Boeotia and Attica. The Athenians moved their families to Salamis, Aegina and Troezen, and the Persian army burned the Acropolis. While the Peloponnesian army walled off the Isthmus of Corinth, the allied fleet lay at Salamis and its commanders argued over whether to fall back to the Isthmus. Themistocles insisted that only the confined waters of Salamis would cancel the enemy's advantage in numbers and seamanship, and threatened to take the Athenian ships and people away to Italy if the others left.

According to Herodotus, Themistocles then sent his slave Sicinnus to tell Xerxes that the Greeks were quarrelling and would slip away in the night. The king had his fleet close the exits of the strait after dark, landed picked troops on the islet of Psyttaleia and, according to Diodorus, sent the Egyptian squadron round the island to block the western channel by Megara. At dawn the Greeks formed their line across the strait from St George's islet to the Kynosoura peninsula, and the Persians rowed in to meet them: Phoenicians on their right facing the Athenians, Ionian Greeks on their left facing the Spartans. Xerxes watched from the slopes of Mount Aigaleo on the mainland.

Crowded into the channel, the Persian ranks lost their order. The Greeks first backed water and then attacked along the whole line. The Persian admiral Ariabignes, a brother of the king, was killed early in the fighting, the Phoenician squadrons were driven onto the Attic shore, and ships in the Persian front that turned to escape fouled those pressing up behind them. As the beaten fleet tried to leave the strait for its base at Phalerum, the Aeginetans waited at the mouth and caught many more, while Aristides led Athenian hoplites onto Psyttaleia and destroyed the Persian force there. Artemisia, queen of Halicarnassus, escaped only by ramming a ship of her own side. The Athenian story that the Corinthians fled north at the start was denied by the other Greeks.

The defeat cost Persia command of the sea. Fearing for his bridges over the Hellespont, Xerxes returned to Asia with much of his army and left Mardonius to continue the war on land; in 479 BC the Greeks destroyed that army at Plataea and the remaining Persian fleet at Mycale. Salamis became a founding story of Athenian sea power, and Aeschylus, who is said to have fought there, made it the subject of The Persians in 472 BC. Much remains debated: the size of the Persian fleet (Herodotus gives 1,207 ships, modern estimates mostly 600 to 800 or fewer), the exact day, whether the Persians entered the strait by night or at dawn, where and how the two lines stood, and whether the Egyptian detour happened at all. In 2017 archaeologists identified the remains of the ancient harbour at Ambelaki where the Greek fleet had gathered.
