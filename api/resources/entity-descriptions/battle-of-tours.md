The Battle of Tours, also called the Battle of Poitiers, was fought on 10 October 732 somewhere in the countryside between Tours and Poitiers in western Gaul. A Frankish army under Charles Martel, Mayor of the Palace of Austrasia, met an Umayyad army from al-Andalus led by the governor Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi. The Franks won, Abd al-Rahman was killed, and the invaders withdrew south over the Pyrenees. The exact field has never been fixed; Moussais-la-Bataille is the traditional candidate, and no source gives the sizes of the armies with certainty. Estimates run from about 15,000 to 30,000 Franks and 20,000 to 30,000 Umayyads.

The road to the battle began with the Umayyad conquest of Hispania and raids across the Pyrenees. In 732 an army pushed through Aquitaine, defeated Duke Odo on the Garonne and near Bordeaux, and then turned toward the Loire. Its cavalry had outrun its supply wagons, and it broke into raiding parties while the main body followed more slowly. One possible aim was the abbey of Saint Martin at Tours, the richest and holiest shrine in the West. Odo appealed to the Franks, and Charles agreed to help only once Odo accepted Frankish overlordship. Charles assembled Austrasian Franks and auxiliaries from beyond the Rhine, including Thuringians, Alemanni and Bavarians, and marched south away from the old Roman roads so as to surprise the enemy.

The Umayyads were caught off guard to find a large army across their path. Charles did not attack. He chose high ground with trees in front and formed his men into a dense, phalanx-like block that Arab sources call a square. For about a week the two armies skirmished while the Umayyads waited for the rest of their strength and tried to judge Frankish numbers, which the forest helped to hide. On the seventh day, with winter coming, Abd al-Rahman ordered his cavalry to charge. They had to climb the slope and pick their way through trees, and they charged repeatedly through the day. Arab accounts say the horsemen broke into the Frankish formation more than once, yet it closed again and held. Abd al-Rahman was killed, and the Umayyad army gave up the attempt and retreated.

The Franks did not pursue far. Charles went on to expand his power in the south, and after Odo's death around 735 his son Hunald took the duchy and acknowledged Charles as overlord. A new Umayyad governor, Uqba ibn al-Hajjaj, invaded in 735, took Arles and Avignon, and held parts of Provence and Septimania for several years; Charles campaigned against him in 736 and 739. No large Umayyad army crossed the Pyrenees again, though raids continued. The victory raised Charles's standing and is often tied to the later rise of the Carolingian dynasty.

How much the battle mattered is still argued. Older Western writers saw it as the moment that saved Europe, and Gibbon and Hallam helped fix that picture. Others see it as one clash in a long frontier struggle, and a raid rather than a full conquest. Muslim chroniclers gave it less weight than the Franks did. Even the sizes of the armies, and the claim that the Franks were outnumbered, rest on thin evidence and are disputed.
