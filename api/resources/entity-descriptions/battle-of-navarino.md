The Battle of Navarino was fought on 20 October 1827 in Navarino Bay, on the south-west coast of the Peloponnese, between a combined British, French and Russian fleet and the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet of Ibrahim Pasha. It was the last major battle fought entirely by sailing ships. In about four hours the Ottoman and Egyptian fleet was wrecked, while the Allies lost 181 killed and 480 wounded and not one ship.

The three powers had agreed in the Treaty of London to press for an armistice in the Greek War of Independence, and Vice-Admiral Edward Codrington was told to enforce it, using force only as a last resort. The Ottomans rejected the terms at the end of August, and the Greeks accepted them in early September. An Ottoman and Egyptian fleet that left Alexandria in August gathered at Navarino, a natural harbour about five kilometres long and sheltered by the islet of Sphacteria. Codrington arrived off the bay on 12 September and negotiated an uneasy ceasefire with Ibrahim on 25 September. It soon broke down, because Greek forces under British commanders kept up operations while Ibrahim's troops devastated Messinia. Codrington turned Ibrahim's attempts to send ships to Patras back to port in early October. On 13 October French and Russian squadrons under Henri de Rigny and Lodewijk van Heiden joined him, and on 18 October the three admirals decided that a winter blockade was impracticable and that they would anchor inside the bay to compel obedience by a show of force.

The Ottoman fleet lay in a horseshoe of three lines, devised with the help of the French officer Letellier, running from the fort at Navarino to the Sphacteria battery, with fireships on the ends. At about 2 p.m. on 20 October the Allies entered by the wide southern channel, the British and French in one column, the Russians slightly behind. The British anchored in the centre, the French opposite the Egyptian wing and the Russians on the western end. Fighting began at the entrance when Ottoman crews fired on a boat sent to stop a fireship, and Allied and Ottoman accounts differ over who started it. The Allies had far heavier guns and better crews. Scipion survived a fireship, Sirene destroyed the frigate Ihsania, Asia disabled the Ottoman flagship and Guerriere, and Azov took 153 hits while destroying several frigates. By about 4 p.m. the front line was finished, and within two more hours most of the rest was sunk or burned, often by its own crews.

Of the 78 Ottoman and Egyptian vessels only eight remained seaworthy. Letellier gave about 3,000 killed and 1,109 wounded, though Codrington suspected the figures should be reversed. The battle broke the sultan's naval power in the Mediterranean and made Greek independence far harder to reverse, although Britain was uneasy about an outcome that helped Russia, and Codrington was later recalled amid controversy over whether he had exceeded his orders.
