name: Battle of the Teutoburg Forest
---
In early September of 9 AD, a Roman army led by the governor Publius Quinctilius Varus was destroyed in north-western Germany by an alliance of Germanic tribes under Arminius, a Cherusci noble who had served in the Roman auxiliaries. Three legions, the XVII, XVIII and XIX, along with their auxiliaries and baggage, were lost. Estimates of Roman dead run from 15,000 to 20,000. The fighting lasted several days, from 8 to 11 September by the usual dating.

Rome had pushed its frontier forward from the Rhine for about two decades, and Varus, an experienced administrator with ties to the imperial family, was sent to turn the region into a province. He spent the summer of 9 with his army at a camp west of the Weser, probably near modern Minden, holding court rather than campaigning. Arminius, who rode with his Cherusci in the Roman train and counted as a trusted adviser, used the time to build a coalition. In July his allies began raiding and he persuaded Varus to split off detachments. A warning from his own father-in-law Segestes was dismissed. In late summer, word of a fabricated revolt led Varus to take a shorter road to the Rhine through Angrivarian land, as Arminius advised.

On 7 September the army mustered, was paid and moved out, and that evening Arminius left to raise his allies. Over the next day the Roman column, strung out over 15 to 20 kilometres of forest track, was harassed by the Bructeri. A heavy downpour forced Varus to halt and entrench. The army then marched through the night and came to Kalkriese, where a sandy corridor about 100 metres wide lay between a wooded hill and the edge of the Great Bog. A ditch and a long zigzag wall of turf and sand, built beforehand, let the Germanic warriors strike from cover. The Romans failed to storm it. The cavalry commander Numonius Vala rode off with the horse and was caught and killed. Varus took his own life, as did other officers, and the remaining army was cut down.

The consequences were large. Augustus is reported to have been shaken, the numbers of the three lost legions were never used again, and replacement legions were rushed to the Rhine. Arminius sent Varus's head to Maroboduus of the Marcomanni, who refused an alliance and forwarded it to Rome for burial. Roman armies under Tiberius and later Germanicus returned across the Rhine, but Rome gave up the idea of a province up to the Elbe.

The site was long uncertain. Many theories, from the Lippe country to the Münsterland, competed for centuries. In 1987 a British amateur archaeologist found coins and sling bolts at Kalkriese, north of Osnabrück, and later digs found the wall, debris concentrated in front of it, and countermarked Varus coins. Most scholars now accept Kalkriese as the scene of at least a major part of the fight, though some debate remains over whether it was the final stand or one episode of a longer running battle. The strengths of both sides, the camp site and the exact route are estimates.
