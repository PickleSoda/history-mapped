The Battle of Ain Jalut was fought on 3 September 1260, which was 26 Ramadan 658 in the Islamic calendar, at a perennial spring on the north-western foot of Mount Gilboa in the Jezreel valley. A Mamluk army from Egypt under Sultan Qutuz, with the commander Baybars at the head of its vanguard, defeated a Mongol force under the general Kitbuqa. The Mongols were backed by Georgian and Armenian vassals and by the levies of two Ayyubid princes. It is remembered as the first major defeat the Mongols suffered in the field in the western Islamic world, and it halted their advance into the Levant.

The background was the great Mongol offensive led by Hulegu, who had destroyed the Abbasid caliphate at Baghdad and then taken Aleppo and Damascus. When news of the death of the Great Khan called him away, Hulegu withdrew with the bulk of his army, leaving Kitbuqa with a single tumen, nominally 10,000 men but usually fewer, and some vassal troops. Hulegu had also sent envoys to Cairo demanding submission, and Qutuz had them killed and their heads displayed at a Cairo gate. Qutuz left Cairo on 26 July, secured Gaza through Baybars, and obtained the neutrality of the Frankish rulers of Acre, who allowed his army to pass and supplied it. Kitbuqa, hearing in the Biqa valley that the Mamluks were coming, gathered his scattered men and took a position near the spring, where Gilboa protected a flank and the stream gave water.

The Mongols arrived first and had their camp in place when Baybars reached a height overlooking it. Qutuz hid the main body of his army in the highlands and used Baybars with a smaller force as bait. The Mongols advanced, and for many hours Baybars attacked and fell back to provoke them without risking his army. Then he feigned a last retreat, and Kitbuqa, irritated by the repeated flight, led his men after him into the hills. The hidden Mamluk troops rose, showered the pursuers with arrows and charged, surrounding them. Timothy May has suggested that the defection of the Syrian allies was a key moment. The Mongols fought desperately to break out, and nearly destroyed the Mamluk left wing. Qutuz threw off his helmet, shouted for God's help and led his guard into the gap. The Mongols were pushed back toward Beisan, rallied and counterattacked, but the day had turned, and Kitbuqa and almost all of the Mongol force remaining in the region died.

The consequences were swift. Damascus fell to the Mamluks five days later and Aleppo within a month, and a Mongol force of about 6,000 was beaten at Homs, ending the first Mongol expedition into Syria. Hulegu ordered the execution of the captive Ayyubid ruler An-Nasir Yusuf. On the road back to Cairo, Qutuz was murdered by emirs in a plot led by Baybars, who took the sultanate and later conquered the last Crusader states, finished by 1291. Hulegu was kept from taking revenge by his quarrel with Berke, the Muslim khan of the Golden Horde, which became open war in 1263 and signalled the end of a unified Mongol empire. The Mamluks cultivated Berke as an ally.

Much remains uncertain. The numbers are disputed: one reconstruction gives Kitbuqa 10,000 to 12,000 men, while a Mamluk chronicler claims 100,000, probably an exaggeration. The accounts of the ambush and the defection of the Syrian allies are partly reconstruction, and the precise positions of the hidden Mamluk divisions are not recorded.
