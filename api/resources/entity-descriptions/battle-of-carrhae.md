The Battle of Carrhae was fought in 53 BC, traditionally on 9 June, near the town of Carrhae, today Harran in southeastern Turkey. A Roman army under Marcus Licinius Crassus, perhaps 42,000 strong with seven legions of heavy infantry, was destroyed by a Parthian force of about 10,000 commanded by the general Surena. Only around a thousand of the Parthians were armoured cataphracts; the rest were horse archers. Plutarch, whose account underlies almost everything we know, puts Roman losses at some 20,000 dead and 10,000 captured, with the Parthian cost described as minimal.

Crassus, one of the three most powerful men in Rome, wanted a military triumph to set beside those of Pompey and Caesar, and in 55 BC he took up the province of Syria and prepared to invade Parthia without the Senate's authority. After an easy first season in Mesopotamia he wintered in Syria. Twice the Armenian king Artavasdes offered him cavalry and a route through the hills where horsemen could not easily manoeuvre, and twice he declined. In spring he marched through Mesopotamia instead. The Parthian king Orodes took most of his army north against Armenia and left Surena with an all-cavalry force to delay the invaders. A local chieftain, Ariamnes, guided Crassus and urged him forward, falsely claiming the Parthians were weak. Plutarch says he led the army into a waterless waste; a modern reading holds that the army simply followed a caravan track beside the Balikh river, where water was plentiful in spring.

On the morning of 9 June the Roman scouts were found dead and a large cavalry force came into view. Cassius advised the usual order of legions in the centre and horse on the wings. Crassus agreed, then changed his mind and drew the army into a hollow square, twelve cohorts to a side, which could not be outflanked but could hardly move. The army reached a stream, where the senior officers wanted to camp; Publius, Crassus's son, argued for immediate battle and prevailed. Surena frightened the Romans with massed hollow drums and then had the cataphracts cast off the cloths that hid their armour. Judging that a charge would not yet break the square, he sent his horse archers round it, outside javelin range. The Roman skirmishers were driven back by arrows. When the legionaries locked shields to ward off the arrows, the cataphracts charged them, and when the Romans opened ranks, the archers shot into the gaps. Crassus hoped the Parthians would run out of arrows, but a train of about a thousand camels kept them supplied.

Crassus then sent Publius with 1,300 Gallic horse, 500 archers and eight cohorts to drive the archers away. They feigned flight, drew the detachment out of reach of the main army, and then the cataphracts closed in while the archers cut off the retreat. The Gauls fought hard but were poorly armed against the Parthians. They withdrew to a hill, where Publius and his officers took their own lives and only 500 men were captured. Crassus advanced to help and was shown his son's head on a spear. The barrage continued until dark. That night Crassus, shattered, ordered a disorganised retreat to Carrhae and left some 4,000 wounded behind to be killed next morning; four cohorts lost their way and were surrounded on a hill, with 20 survivors. The next day Surena offered a truce in return for the Roman territories east of the Euphrates. Crassus hesitated, but his men threatened to mutiny, and at the meeting a scuffle broke out in which he and his officers were killed. Most of the remaining Romans were captured or killed, though Cassius led about 10,000 men back to Syria.

Carrhae ended the First Triumvirate and left Rome's eastern frontier exposed. Surena was executed by the jealous Orodes soon afterwards, and Parthian forces later raided Syria and Armenia. The defeat taught the Romans that unsupported legions in open country could not match Parthian mobility. Details remain disputed. The exact site is unknown, the account of Ariamnes's treachery is doubted, and Plutarch lays the blame on Crassus more heavily than some modern historians would, who stress the weakness of the Roman system as a whole and the particular failure of the night retreat.
