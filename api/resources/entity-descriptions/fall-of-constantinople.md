name: Fall of Constantinople
---
Between 6 April and 29 May 1453 the Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II besieged and captured Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire. The city, set on a triangular peninsula between the Sea of Marmara and the Golden Horn, was by then a small and impoverished remnant of the old Roman world, ruled by Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos. Its fall ended an empire that traced its line back to Rome and opened the way for the Ottomans to make the city their own capital.

The background was a long decline. Byzantine lands had shrunk to the city itself and a few holdings in Greece, while the Ottomans held both shores of the straits. The defenders numbered about eight thousand, including some two thousand Western volunteers, Genoese under Giovanni Giustiniani, Venetians, Catalans and others, and had around twenty-six ships. Mehmed brought an army that Western sources put near a hundred thousand, a fleet of perhaps a hundred and ten ships by the more careful modern estimate, and a train of heavy guns, among them the giant bombard cast by the Hungarian founder Orban. The defenders' best asset was the great Theodosian land wall, about five and a half kilometres long, together with a boom chain closing the Golden Horn.

The Ottoman army camped before the land walls on 2 April, with Mehmed's tent near the Mesoteichion, where the Lycus valley made the wall weakest. The guns battered the wall for weeks, but they fired slowly and the defenders repaired the damage each night. Costly assaults failed. At sea the fleet could not pass the chain, and on 20 April four Christian ships fought their way in. Mehmed's answer, on 22 April, was to drag ships over the hill of Galata on greased logs into the Golden Horn, forcing the defenders to spread thinner. A fireship attempt on 28 April failed, and prisoners on both sides were killed in sight of the walls. In mid-May Serbian miners dug under the walls, but the German engineer Johannes Grant's countermines defeated them. Mehmed offered terms on 21 May, Constantine refused, and a council overruled the vizier Halil Pasha, who wanted to give up.

The final assault began shortly after midnight on 29 May. Waves of Christian vassal troops, irregular azaps and Anatolian Turkmen wore down the defenders, and then the Janissaries attacked. Giustiniani was badly wounded and carried away, and the loss of his leadership shook the defence. The Ottomans broke through at several points, with Ulubatli Hasan among the first on the wall, and the city fell within hours. Constantine died fighting, though accounts differ and the exact manner of his death is unknown. Soldiers took the city's houses and the people sheltering in Hagia Sophia; about 4,000 Greeks were killed and some 30,000 enslaved. A few Venetian and Genoese ships escaped with refugees.

Mehmed made the city his capital and it grew into Istanbul. The fall is often used to mark the end of the Middle Ages, and Greek scholars who fled westward fed the Renaissance, though historians argue over how much weight such a date deserves. Debate continues about the real size of the armies, how effective the great guns were, whether the Orban gun burst, and how Constantine met his end.
