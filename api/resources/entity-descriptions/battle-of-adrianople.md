The Battle of Adrianople was fought on 9 August 378 a few hours' march from the Thracian city of Adrianople, now Edirne in Turkey. A Gothic and Alan force under Fritigern, together with the cavalry of Alatheus and Saphrax, defeated the field army of the eastern Roman emperor Valens. Valens died, his body was never found, and perhaps two thirds of his army was lost. It was the worst Roman defeat since the third-century crisis.

The roots lay in 376, when Goths displaced by the Huns, led by Alavivus and Fritigern, asked to settle inside the empire. Valens admitted them as allies, hoping for farmers and recruits, but the provincial commanders Lupicinus and Maximus cheated and starved them, and the Goths rose in revolt. Two years of inconclusive fighting followed, with reinforcements sent by the western emperor Gratian. In 378 Valens came from Antioch, reached Constantinople on 30 May, and moved to Adrianople after Sebastianus had won some small successes. Gratian, meanwhile, had to deal with an Alamannic attack on the Rhine.

Scouts told Valens that around ten thousand Goths were advancing from the north. He fortified his camp at Adrianople. Richomeres brought Gratian's request to wait, and Valens' officers agreed, but Valens chose to act alone. On 8 August Fritigern offered peace in return for land, and Valens refused, partly because his estimate of the enemy ignored the Gothic horse that were away foraging.

On the morning of 9 August Valens marched out, leaving the treasury and administration in the city, and arrived around noon after about eight miles over rough ground. His men were tired and thirsty. The Goths held a hill behind a circle of wagons holding their families and set fires to harass the Romans with smoke. Talks about hostages dragged on, which suited Fritigern. Then some Roman units attacked unordered, first the imperial scholae under the Iberian prince Bacurius, who were pushed back, then the left wing, which reached the wagons too late. At that moment the Gothic cavalry returned and enveloped the disordered Romans, who were driven back against the foot of the hill, unable to manoeuvre in heavy armour and long shields. The army broke, and killing continued until nightfall. Valens' guards left him; one tradition says he burned in a cottage set alight by Goths who did not know who he was.

Modern estimates put each army at about 12,000 to 15,000, with Roman losses of 10,000 to 20,000. Sebastianus and many officers died, a third of the army escaped, and the Goths then tried and failed to take Adrianople. The defeat destroyed the core of the eastern field army and armouries across the Danube provinces, yet it did not end the empire. Valens' successor Theodosius I eventually accepted the Goths as allies, and they remained a distinct people inside the frontiers.

Debate continues over the site, since three locations have been proposed, near Muratcali, west of the Tonzos, or near Demirhanli, and over the cavalry. Charles Oman saw a turning point in which horsemen overcame the legions, but T. S. Burns and later historians stress that the fight was chiefly between infantry, with the Gothic cavalry decisive only at the critical moment.
