The Second Battle of El Alamein was fought from 23 October to 4 November 1942 around the Egyptian railway halt of El Alamein, between the British Eighth Army under Bernard Montgomery and the German-Italian Panzer Army under Erwin Rommel. It ended in an Allied victory. The Axis army was broken, driven out of Egypt and Libya and pushed back to the borders of Tunisia, and the threat to Egypt, the Middle East and Iran was lifted for good.

The battle followed two earlier clashes at the same place. After the First Battle of El Alamein and the Battle of Alam el Halfa had stopped the Axis advance, Rommel went onto the defensive. His supply line from Tripoli was extremely long, fuel was scarce because the Eastern Front took priority, and he was ill, so Georg Stumme took over for a time. The Panzer Army dug in behind at least two belts of mines about five kilometres apart, linked into boxes the British called devil's gardens, with roughly half a million mines laid. Rommel alternated German and Italian infantry in the forward line and, unusually, split his armour into a northern and a southern group. The Eighth Army, supplied from Britain, India, Australia, New Zealand and the United States, had about 195,000 men and over a thousand tanks against some 116,000 men and about 547 tanks.

Montgomery's plan, Operation Lightfoot, called for a main attack in the north by XXX Corps and a diversion in the south, with X Corps armour then passing through two cleared corridors. The attack opened on the night of 23 October under a huge barrage, but the lanes through the mines took far longer to clear than planned. Stumme died of a heart attack on 24 October, and Rommel returned on the 25th. A tank clash at dusk near the Kidney feature cost both sides heavily without changing the line. Montgomery then shifted his effort north toward the coast, where the 9th Australian Division took Point 29 and kept attacking the coastal salient. Rommel pulled his armour north, which his scant fuel made irreversible. His last attempt to seize the initiative, on 28 October, was broken up by artillery and air attack.

The decisive blow was Operation Supercharge, launched at 01:05 on 2 November behind an air bombardment and a barrage of 15,000 shells. Infantry cleared a path toward Tel el Aqqaqir, and the 9th Armoured Brigade charged the gun line at dawn, losing most of its tanks but wrecking the anti-tank screen. By 4 November the Axis front had a hole some 19 kilometres wide, the Ariete, Littorio and Trieste divisions were destroyed, and Rommel asked Hitler for leave to retreat to Fuka. The Allied pursuit was slowed by congested tracks and an early failure to trap the 90th Light Division. Operation Torch landed in French North Africa on 8 November.

Axis casualty figures vary widely, from about 41,800 to 73,000, with some 30,000 prisoners counted by 11 November, against 13,560 for the Eighth Army. Historians still discuss how far Montgomery's slow, methodical approach cost opportunities in the pursuit, and how much Ultra intelligence and the sinking of Axis fuel tankers shaped the outcome beside the weight of Allied material.
