The Battle of Plassey was fought on 23 June 1757 near Palashi, on the bank of the Bhagirathi, the branch of the Hooghly that runs through western Bengal. A small army of the East India Company under Robert Clive, roughly 3,100 men with eight field guns, faced the host of Siraj ud-Daulah, Nawab of Bengal, which the sources put at around 50,000 with a large artillery park and about fifty French gunners under St. Frais. By the evening the Nawab's camp was in British hands and its army had scattered. The day cost the Company around 22 to 27 killed and 50 wounded, and Clive estimated the Nawab's loss at about 500.

The background was a quarrel between a young ruler and a trading company that had become a military power. Siraj ud-Daulah resented the Company's fortifications and the abuse of its trading privileges, and in 1756 he took Calcutta. The Company's response from Madras, tied to the wider Anglo-French rivalry in India, recovered the city and then turned to politics. Clive built a conspiracy with Mir Jafar, who commanded part of the Nawab's army, with the banking house of the Jagat Seths and other courtiers, promising Mir Jafar the throne in return for money and territory. A treaty followed, though the intermediary Omichund was later cheated of his promised share.

Clive marched from Chandernagar in mid-June, took Katwa, and then faced a hard decision. A council of war on 21 June voted thirteen to seven against attacking at once, but after an hour alone Clive ordered the crossing of the river for the next morning. He reached the mango grove beyond Plassey at one in the morning on 23 June and made his quarters in a nearby hunting lodge. At dawn the Nawab's army came out in a great arc, with Mir Jafar on the wing nearest the British. The first French shot came at eight, a long and mostly harmless cannonade followed, and Clive drew his men back behind the grove's bank.

Heavy rain soaked the Nawab's powder but not the British, whose gunners had covered theirs. When Mir Madan led the cavalry in a charge he was mortally wounded by grapeshot. Siraj, shaken, was advised to retire, and he left the field with an escort. At about two the army turned back to its entrenchment. Major Kilpatrick advanced on the French position without orders, Clive followed with the whole force, and in the afternoon the tank, the hill east of the redoubt and the redoubt itself fell, the last two to Major Coote's grenadiers and a general assault. By five the British held the camp.

The consequences were far larger than the fighting. Siraj was captured at Rajmahal and killed by Mir Jafar's son, and Mir Jafar was installed as Nawab, with the Company receiving land near Calcutta and a large indemnity. French influence in Bengal faded, and the Company's hold on the province's revenue became the base for its later power in India. Historians still argue over how much the battle was won by arms and how much by treachery, since much of the Nawab's army never fought, and the exact positions of the field works are reconstructed because the river has moved since.
