name: Battle of Thermopylae
---
The Battle of Thermopylae was fought in the late summer of 480 BC, between an alliance of Greek city-states and the army of Xerxes I of the Achaemenid Empire. It took place at the Hot Gates, a narrow strip of ground between the cliffs of Mt Kallidromos and the Malian Gulf, on the road from Thessaly into central Greece. Sources disagree on the date: it is placed on 21 to 23 July, on 20 August or on 8 to 10 September. The Persians won, and the pass was opened to their advance into Phocis, Boeotia and Attica.

Xerxes had gathered a vast force to avenge the defeat of his father Darius at Marathon and to subdue the Greeks. Herodotus put the whole invasion at millions of men, but modern scholars generally think in terms of 120,000 to 300,000, and a few propose far less, around 40,000 or 65,000. The Greeks sent a covering force under Leonidas I, king of Sparta, while their fleet fought a parallel action off Artemisium. The standard modern count of the defenders is about 7,000, made up of 300 Spartans, other Peloponnesians, 700 Thespians, 400 Thebans, Locrians, Phocians and perhaps some helots. The geography favoured them. The pass was probably about 100 metres wide, a Phocian wall stood at the middle gate, and a marsh made by hot springs added to the obstacle.

For four days Xerxes waited. On the fifth he opened with 5,000 archers, whose arrows were largely stopped by bronze helmets and shields, and then sent in 10,000 Medes and Cissians in waves. Fighting by city contingents and rotating their men, the Greeks held the front and inflicted heavy losses. In the afternoon the king committed his Immortals, with little more success, and the second day went the same way. Then a local man, Ephialtes of Trachis, told Xerxes of a mountain track, and Hydarnes was sent along it at night with a flanking column, which Diodorus puts at 20,000 men.

At dawn on the third day the Phocians guarding the track were brushed aside. Leonidas, warned by a runner, dismissed the bulk of his army, and the 300 Spartans stayed with the Thespians under Demophilus and the Thebans. A rearguard blocking the pass allowed more than 3,000 men to get away and avoided a cavalry pursuit on open ground. The Greeks advanced out of the wall into the wider part of the pass and fought until their spears broke. Leonidas fell, and Persian archers and the Immortals closed in. The Thebans surrendered. The rest withdrew to a hill, probably Kolonos, where Marinatos found Persian arrowheads in 1939, and were shot down.

The defence cost Xerxes several days and, by Herodotus' account, up to 20,000 men, though that figure is certainly inflated, as is his 4,000 Greek dead. The Greek fleet withdrew from Artemisium, Athens was evacuated and later burned, and the issue was settled at Salamis and Plataea. What is debated includes the true size of the armies, the exact date, the reasons the Thebans stayed, and whether Leonidas acted out of Spartan custom, an oracle or tactical calculation. The battle became an enduring symbol of courage against odds, and the epitaph attributed to Simonides still marks the site.
