The influenza pandemic of 1918 to 1920, often misleadingly called the Spanish flu, was one of the deadliest pandemics in history. Caused by an H1N1 influenza A virus, it infected perhaps 500 million people, around a third of the world's population, and killed somewhere between 17 and 50 million, possibly more. It struck in the last year of the First World War, and the movement of armies, troopships and labourers carried it around the world within months.

The first well-documented cases came from the United States: rural Haskell County in Kansas reported a severe influenza in early 1918, and on 4 March a cook at Camp Funston, a large army training camp, fell ill, followed within days by hundreds of soldiers. Where the virus first arose is still unknown. Some researchers favour a North American origin, others the British army camp at Étaples in France, Austria, or northern China, where a respiratory illness in late 1917 preceded the sending of Chinese labourers to France. The name Spanish flu came from neutral Spain, whose newspapers reported the disease freely while the warring countries censored the news.

The pandemic came in waves. The first, in the spring and early summer of 1918, spread from the American camps to France and across Europe, Asia and North Africa, sickening armies but killing relatively few. The second, from late August 1918, was far more lethal. It probably spread on ships from Brest to Boston and to Freetown in Sierra Leone, followed the shipping lanes to every continent, and peaked in October, the deadliest month of the pandemic. India lost at least 12 million people and South Africa about 300,000 in six weeks; in Western Samoa, reached by a ship from New Zealand, nine in ten people fell ill and nearly a third of adult men died. A third wave in early 1919 reached Australia, which a maritime quarantine had shielded until then, and further outbreaks continued into 1920.

The disease was unusual in how hard it hit young adults, though the extent of this is debated, and many victims died of bacterial pneumonia after a long illness. Overcrowded camps and hospitals, wartime malnutrition and the lack of antibiotics made it worse. Communities closed schools and banned gatherings, with mixed success. The pandemic shaped later public health planning, and its virus, recovered from preserved tissue in the 1990s and 2000s, remains the reference case for studying pandemic influenza.
