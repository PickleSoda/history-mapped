The Battle of Blenheim was fought on 13 August 1704 beside the Danube in Bavaria, around the village of Blindheim, which English speakers called Blenheim. A Grand Alliance army of some 52,000 men under the Duke of Marlborough and Prince Eugene of Savoy attacked a Franco-Bavarian army of about 56,000 under Marshal Tallard, Marshal Marsin and the Elector Maximilian II Emanuel of Bavaria. The Allies won decisively: Tallard was captured, the Blindheim garrison surrendered, and Bavaria was soon knocked out of the War of the Spanish Succession.

The campaign began when Marlborough marched an English and Dutch army from the Low Countries to the Danube to save Vienna from a French-Bavarian threat. There he joined Eugene, whose Imperial troops had shadowed the enemy. The French commanders stood on the north bank of the river and were convinced the Allies were about to withdraw towards Nördlingen. Prisoners' reports reinforced that belief, so when the Allied columns marched in from the east before dawn on 13 August, they were met by surprise. By about 06:00 the head of the march reached Schwenningen, three kilometres from Blindheim.

The Franco-Bavarian line ran about six kilometres from the Danube at Blindheim north-west through Oberglauheim to Lutzingen and the pine-covered hills of the Swabian Jura, fronted by the marshy Nebel stream. Tallard held the ground from the river to Oberglauheim, Marsin and the Elector the rest. Cutts was sent against Blindheim, and his repeated assaults panicked Clérambault into packing his reserve battalions into the village, where they could neither fire nor manoeuvre. Eugene attacked Lutzingen three times and was thrown back each time. Marlborough crossed the Nebel on improvised bridges, beat off the Gens d'Armes, and then had to deal with the loss of Holstein-Beck's Dutch brigades at Oberglauheim and Marsin's threat to his flank. Fugger's Imperial cuirassiers, lent by Eugene, drove Marsin's squadrons back.

Just after 17:00 Marlborough sent his cavalry forward in the centre against Tallard's tired horse and nine raw battalions. The French centre gave way, thousands of horsemen fled toward the Danube and Höchstädt, and many drowned. Tallard was taken prisoner, and Eugene's Prussians finally carried Lutzingen as Marsin and the Elector withdrew. The troops left in Blindheim were surrounded and surrendered during the night.

The French lost some 6,000 killed or drowned, 7,000 wounded and over 14,000 captured; the Allies lost around 4,500 killed and 8,000 wounded. The victory ended the long run of French successes, removed Bavaria from the war and secured Vienna. Debate continues over how much credit belongs to Marlborough's planning against French errors, above all Tallard's decision to let the Allies cross the Nebel and Clérambault's crowding of Blindheim. The map's unit positions and the schematic west course of the Nebel and the Danube are reconstructions.
