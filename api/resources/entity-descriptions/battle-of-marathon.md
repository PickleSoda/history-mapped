---
The Battle of Marathon was fought on the coastal plain of Marathon in Attica, about 27 kilometres north-east of Athens, in the late summer of 490 BC. By the conventional dating it fell on 12 September, although some scholars, allowing for a Spartan calendar a month ahead of the Athenian one, would put it a month earlier. An Athenian army reinforced by Plataea faced a seaborne force of the Achaemenid Empire under Datis and Artaphernes. The Athenians won, and the expedition that Darius I had sent to punish Athens turned back toward Asia.

The expedition grew out of the Ionian revolt, in which Athens and Eretria had helped the Greek cities of Asia Minor rebel against Persian rule. Darius sent a fleet which first took Eretria, then landed at the bay of Marathon on the advice of Hippias, the exiled tyrant of Athens, who hoped to be restored to power. The plain offered room for cavalry and a long beach for the ships. Under the guidance of Miltiades, the Athenian general with the most experience of fighting Persians, the army marched out quickly and took position on a spur of Mount Agrieliki, blocking the routes toward Athens. Pheidippides ran to Sparta for help, but the Spartans could not march until the full moon because of a religious festival. A contingent of about a thousand Plataean hoplites joined the Athenians, a gesture the Athenians never forgot.

The armies faced each other for around five days. Time favoured the Athenians, who were waiting for the Spartans, while the Persians were reluctant to attack hoplites on ground of their choosing. Herodotus gives no army sizes. Later ancient writers give about 9,000 or 10,000 Athenians and 1,000 Plataeans, which modern historians accept, and modern estimates put the Persian infantry at perhaps 25,000 with roughly 1,000 horse. The ancient accounts of what ended the stalemate differ. One theory, based on the Suda, holds that the Persian cavalry had left or were re-embarking, giving the Athenians a chance. Another holds that the Persians finally advanced and the Athenians responded by attacking.

When the Athenians formed up, Miltiades drew the two tribes of the centre, Leontis under Themistocles and Antiochis under Aristides, four ranks deep and the rest eight, so that the line matched the longer Persian one. The Athenians advanced and, once within the zone of the Persian arrows, ran at the enemy. In the centre the Persians and the Sakae pushed the thin Greek line back inland, but on both wings the Athenians and Plataeans routed the Persian levies. Rather than chase them, they turned inward and fell on the Persian centre from both sides. The Persians broke and ran for their ships, some into the marsh, where an unknown number drowned. The Athenians took seven ships; Cynaegirus, a brother of the playwright Aeschylus, lost a hand holding one and died. Herodotus counts 6,400 Persian dead against 192 Athenians, among them the war archon Callimachus and the general Stesilaus.

The Persian fleet then sailed around Cape Sounion toward Athens, whether after the battle or, as some modern historians argue, as the reason for it. The army marched back at once, leaving the two centre tribes under Aristides to guard the field, and reached the city first. The Persians gave up and went home. The Spartans arrived the following day, having covered about 220 kilometres in three days, and agreed that the Athenians had won a great victory. The dead were buried on the battlefield in tumuli. Darius began preparing a larger expedition but Egypt rose in revolt, and he died in 486 BC. His son Xerxes invaded in 480 BC, and the Persian wars ended at Plataea the next year. Marathon became a founding story of Athenian self-confidence, and later tradition gave rise to the marathon run. What remains debated includes the date, the numbers, why the Athenians attacked when they did, and how much of the double envelopment was planned.
