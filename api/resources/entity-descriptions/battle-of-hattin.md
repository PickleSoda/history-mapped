The Battle of Hattin was fought on 3 and 4 July 1187 beside the Horns of Hattin, a double hill west of Tiberias in what is now northern Israel. Saladin, sultan of Egypt and Syria and founder of the Ayyubid dynasty, destroyed the field army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem under King Guy of Lusignan. It was one of the most complete defeats the crusader states ever suffered.

The background was a long, uneasy truce between Saladin and the Frankish kingdom, torn by feuds at court. Raynald of Chatillon was accused by Saladin of breaking the truce, and Saladin gathered the largest army he had ever led, some 40,000 men including about 12,000 regular cavalry. He crossed the Jordan on 30 June. The Franks, reconciled for the moment, mustered every man who could bear arms, perhaps 18,000 to 20,000 including about 1,200 knights, many infantry, Italian crossbowmen and local turcopoles, and they carried the relic of the True Cross. They waited at La Saphorie, where there was good water.

On 2 July Saladin stormed Raymond of Tripoli's town of Tiberias, hoping to bait the Franks into leaving their strong position. That night a council argued over the move. Raymond advised patience, but Gerard de Ridefort and Raynald accused him of cowardice and Guy ordered an advance. On 3 July the army marched east in great heat under constant harassment from Muslim horse archers. The Springs of Turan did not suffice, and at midday the Franks turned toward the springs of Hattin. Saladin's cavalry placed itself between them and the water, and the Franks camped thirsty on the dry plateau near Meskenah. During the night the Muslims chanted, set the grass alight and kept their own men supplied with water brought up from the lake.

On 4 July the Franks, blinded by smoke and showered with arrows, formed up with Raymond leading the van, Guy in the centre and Balian of Ibelin and Joscelin of Edessa in the rear. Raymond charged twice toward the lake; the second charge broke through and he escaped to Tyre. Much of the infantry fled up onto the Horns and took no further part. Guy's knights charged three times against the Muslims around Saladin, and Saladin's son al-Afdal recorded that his father said the Franks were not beaten until Guy's tent fell. It did. Guy, Raynald, Gerard de Ridefort, and many barons were taken. Saladin killed Raynald, or ordered him killed, and spared Guy with the remark that kings do not kill kings. About 200 captured Templars and Hospitallers were beheaded, with the Templar Grand Master spared, and the turcopoles were executed as apostates. Other prisoners were sold as slaves.

The consequences were swift. With the field army gone, the towns and castles of the kingdom were left with few defenders, and Saladin was free to press on against its cities. The True Cross was sent to Damascus, and Guy was freed in 1188. Historians still argue about the numbers on each side, the exact line of the Frankish march and where the night camp stood, and about how much of the catastrophe lay in Guy's decision and how much in Saladin's skilful use of thirst, smoke and mobile archers.
