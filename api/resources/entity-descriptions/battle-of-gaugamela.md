The Battle of Gaugamela was fought on 1 October 331 BC on a broad plain east of the Tigris, in what is now the Kurdistan Region of northern Iraq, between the Macedonian and Greek army of Alexander the Great and the imperial army of the Achaemenid king Darius III. Ancient writers often named it after Arbela, the modern Erbil, where Darius had left his treasure. Outnumbered and overlapped on both flanks, Alexander nevertheless broke the Persian centre and put Darius to flight. The victory cost Persia its last royal field army and opened the heart of the empire to the invader.

After his defeat at Issus in 333 BC, Darius let Alexander take the Levant and Egypt while he gathered a new army in the east: Bactrian, Sogdian and Saca horsemen under Bessus, contingents from Arachosia and India, and the levies of Mesopotamia and Media. Expecting to fight in the open again, he had 200 scythed chariots prepared and longer weapons issued, and he chose a plain near the village of Gaugamela on the Bumodus river, where uneven patches were levelled so that chariots and cavalry could charge freely. Alexander left Egypt in the spring, crossed the Euphrates at Thapsacus and then took a northern route, crossing the Tigris unopposed in mid-September. When his scouts found the Persians, he rested his men, fortified a camp, advanced by night and halted on hills overlooking the plain. He refused Parmenion's advice to attack in the dark, while the Persians, fearing exactly that, stood in their ranks all night.

On the morning of 1 October Alexander drew up some 47,000 men in two lines, with flank guards angled back on both wings, against a Persian line that reached beyond his at either end. Instead of attacking head-on he moved obliquely to the right, toward the edge of the prepared ground. Darius sent the horsemen of his left wing round the Macedonian flank, where a stubborn cavalry fight developed against Alexander's flank guards, and loosed his chariots, which the Macedonian light infantry shot down or let pass through opened files. As more Persian cavalry rode out toward the flank, a gap opened beside the Persian centre. Alexander formed the Companion cavalry into a wedge, led it with the hypaspists and part of the phalanx into the gap and drove at Darius, who fled. The Persian centre and left fell apart behind him.

Elsewhere the outcome hung in the balance for longer. Mazaeus's cavalry on the Persian right had been pressing Parmenion's wing hard, and when two phalanx battalions halted to support it, Persian and Indian horsemen rode through the opening to plunder the Macedonian baggage until the reserve line turned about and drove them off. Answering Parmenion's call for help, Alexander broke off his pursuit and fought the fiercest cavalry action of the day against retreating Parthian, Indian and Persian horse, while the Thessalian cavalry broke Mazaeus's wing. He then chased Darius as far as Arbela, which he reached the next day. Macedonian losses were light, probably hundreds rather than thousands; Persian losses were very heavy, although the ancient totals, from 40,000 to 300,000, cannot be taken as counts.

Gaugamela decided the war. Mazaeus handed over Babylon and was made its satrap, Susa and Persepolis fell within months, and Darius, a fugitive in Media, was murdered by Bessus and his fellow conspirators in the summer of 330 BC. A Babylonian astronomical diary written at the time records the king's defeat and says his troops abandoned him. Much remains debated: the size of the Persian army, which ancient writers put as high as a million men and modern historians mostly between 50,000 and 120,000; the conduct of Parmenion, whom some ancient writers called sluggish that day; and the exact site, which most recent research places on the Navkur plain beside Tell Gomel, a name that seems to preserve Gaugamela, rather than at Karamlesh, about 30 km further south, long the favoured candidate.
