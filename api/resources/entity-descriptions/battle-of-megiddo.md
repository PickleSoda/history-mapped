The Battle of Megiddo was fought in the plain below the tell of Megiddo, at the south-western edge of the Jezreel Valley in what is now northern Israel. The traditional date is 16 April 1457 BC, the 21st day of the first month of the third season in year 23 of Thutmose III, though some scholars put it in 1482 or 1479 BC. On one side stood the Egyptian army under the pharaoh; on the other a coalition of Canaanite and Syrian rulers led by the king of Kadesh, with the prince of Megiddo. It is the earliest battle for which a fairly detailed account survives, and it is often credited with the first recorded body count.

The background was a revolt against Egyptian overlordship. Thutmose had only recently come into full power after the regency of Hatshepsut, and the northern vassals, probably encouraged by Mitanni, hoped to throw off the Egyptian yoke. Megiddo mattered because it guarded the road through the Jezreel Valley, a main link between Egypt and Mesopotamia. The Egyptian army went from the frontier fortress of Tjaru to Gaza, then north to Yehem. The king of Kadesh meanwhile gathered his allies in Megiddo and sent detachments to watch the two safer roads, by Taanach and by Yokneam.

At Yehem the generals faced three choices, and the sources say they urged one of the longer, safer roads. Thutmose chose the short road through the Aruna ravine, a passage where men had to file one behind another and might be cut down at its mouth. The pharaoh went at the head himself, and the column came out onto the plain without opposition. Overnight the Egyptians formed a wide arc facing the city, with the wings reaching towards Taanach and north-west of Megiddo and the king in the centre. Modern estimates give each side about 10,000 infantry and 1,000 chariots, though the Egyptian records give no firm figures.

The attack at dawn broke the coalition quickly. The rebels fled for the walls, the gates were shut, and the fugitives, including the kings, were hauled up by makeshift ropes. The Egyptian troops then stopped to plunder the camp, taking 924 chariots and 200 suits of armour, and the chance to take the city at once was lost. Thutmose had a ditch and a wooden palisade built around Megiddo and the siege lasted about seven months. The king of Kadesh escaped, but Megiddo surrendered and its people were spared. Modern summaries put enemy losses at about 8,300 dead and 3,400 captured, against some 4,000 Egyptian dead, but the Karnak list itself names 340 prisoners.

Thutmose took sons of the defeated rulers to be educated in Egypt, so that they would return as friendly kings. Megiddo was only the beginning of a series of near annual campaigns that carried Egypt to its greatest extent. Everything we know comes from Egyptian records, chiefly the Karnak Annals based on the scribe Tjaneni's journal, so the numbers and the pharaoh's boldness are the court's own account. The later word Armageddon derives from the name Megiddo.
